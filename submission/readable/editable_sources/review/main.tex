\section{Summary}
Dorrell et al.\ (2023, ICLR) ask why the brain would represent two-dimensional position with grid cells. Their answer is that a spatial code should be \emph{actionable}: every movement (a step north, then east, and so on) should change the population activity by the same matrix wherever the animal starts, so that a route never travelled can still be predicted. Representation theory restricts each neuron's tuning to a sum of spatial sinusoids. Learning determines their amplitudes and wave vectors. Gradient descent adjusts these parameters in a 64-neuron population. The objective separates distant locations, subject to non-negative and bounded firing. The optimum, they report, is several modules of roughly hexagonal grid cells. They explain this with analytic and heuristic arguments: non-negativity needs a constant offset or harmonically related frequencies, which gives a lattice of frequencies; the animal's occupancy and the resolution it needs select a ring of useful frequencies that a hexagonal lattice fills most densely; and a small neural length scale $\sigma$ favours non-harmonic frequencies, hence several modules. Three predictions follow (module size against the squared ratio of grid spacing to field width, small offsets between module orientations, and changes with room shape), compared informally with \citet{stensola2012}. We read the optimality claim as a numerically supported heuristic rather than a proof, since the paper's Appendix~G calls the problem non-convex; this is the point in our understanding we are least sure of.

\section{Originality}
Earlier accounts work at other levels. Continuous-attractor models \citep{burak2009} hand-tune a circuit that integrates velocity; efficient-coding analyses, as the authors note, assume grid-like tuning. \citet{sorscher2019} obtain hexagonal grids from non-negativity and a place-cell read-out target and, in the authors' view (Appendix~H.2), modules only through a quantisation effect. Closest is \citet{gao2021}: they also use a position-independent linear update (a group representation), but add an isotropy term that favours hexagons directly and obtain several modules only by imposing block-diagonal updates. Dorrell et al.\ keep the linear action, drop the isotropy term, derive hexagons from the band of useful spatial frequencies, and derive modules from~$\sigma$. The contributions are an interpretable family of codes, modules without imposed block structure, and quantitative predictions.

The weaknesses follow from the framework. Requiring a location-independent \emph{linear} action is a substantive restriction, so periodic tuning is largely a consequence of the formulation, and ``grid cells from minimal constraints'' means hexagonal and modular codes \emph{within} linear-action models. The claim that isotropy is unnecessary (Appendix~H.1) is argued but never tested by running the authors' objective with and without it, and there is no head-to-head comparison with \citet{sorscher2019}.

\emph{Level of abstraction.} The paper is computational: it has no spiking, learning rule, velocity input or noise. The model is more abstract than attractor or trained-network models. It covers variables whose transformations form a group, including the circle, sphere and 3-D space (Appendix~M). Its linear update rule is more restrictive. We agree with this choice for a ``why'' question, because it lets the authors derive rather than fit. The cost is that the link to attractor circuits, which they leave as future work, stays open.

\section{Clarity}
The paper is well organised. Section~2 introduces a circle before the plane. Figure~4A explains the frequency-ring argument, and Figure~6 shows every neuron. The authors acknowledge simplifications (``this is a bad loss'', Section~3.1). Our difficulties were specific:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt,parsep=0pt]
\item Table~B.3 gives the settings of Figure~2B only. Figures~8 and~9 change $\sigma$, $l$ and the number of steps and rescale the initial constraint weights with the loss size, without giving values.
\item The number of frequencies $D$ and the initial variances (``as in Table~B.3'') are not in the table.
\item The norm target $k_n=4$ is a log-target (equation~26), but the equation~25 penalty is an average squared error that is small when the constraint holds. A target of $e^{4}\approx55$ exceeded the measured norm penalties and, in our runs, left the constraint inactive (see Reproducibility).
\item Equation~22 normalises by a \emph{sum} over $M$ sampled points while the main text uses expectations, so the numerical meaning of $\sigma$ depends on $M$.
\item Figure~9 counts ``modules'' and Figure~8 judges ``hexagonal'' without saying how, and the Figure~2B caption omits $\sigma$, $N$ and $l$.
\item Figure~5 holds nine panels, and its comparisons with data (5B, 5E, 5I) are by eye, without an effect size.
\end{enumerate}
Inspection of the public code (commit 940659e, \href{https://github.com/WilburDoz/ICLR_Actionable_Reps}{authors' repository}) found that its norm routine aggregates unnormalised responses where equation~25 uses normalised ones, and that its \texttt{sigma\_sq} argument must be distinguished from the paper's $\sigma$. These differences motivate per-figure configuration files. They do not establish errors in the reported results.

\section{Quality}
\emph{Strengths.} Each ingredient is tied to a mechanism that makes a checkable statement: non-negativity forces harmonics and a constant offset, the two spatial scales select a ring of frequencies, and $\sigma$ sets how many modules arise. The last held in our runs: at $l=0.5$ the number of modules rose from \sigmaLargeModules{} at $\sigma=\sigmaLarge$ to \sigmaSmallModules{} at $\sigma=\sigmaSmall$ (Table~\ref{tab:verdict}). The authors also volunteer limits (Appendix~B.4.2: ``the more modules the less hexagonal''; their data comparisons are ``rudimentary'').

\emph{Weaknesses.} (1) Optimality is heuristic: Appendix~G calls the problem non-convex, and the evidence is gradient descent from random starts, which varies with the seed. At $\sigma=0.2$, nine 50k-step runs gave \baseModules{}$\pm$\baseModulesSd{} modules with \baseHex{} hexagonal per run on average; three runs at the paper's 150k steps gave \mainLowModulesA, \mainLowModulesB{} and \mainLowModulesC{} modules, none hexagonal. We did not reproduce Figure~2B under our documented conventions; this does not establish how representative the authors' result was. Even so, ``several hexagonal modules'' is the weakest part of the central result: the authors concede that more modules are less hexagonal (quoted above), and the main text shows a single clean population. (2) ``Minimal'' rests on ablations on a small torus (Appendix~I). On the plane, removing non-negativity or the functional term destroyed grid structure in our runs, but removing actionability did not (\ablNoActGrid{} of cells still scored above 0.3), so a grid score alone cannot separate lattices from bumpy patterns. (3) The hexagonal outcome appears to depend on how the bounded-activity constraint is enforced (Reproducibility). Appendix~B.4 says dependence on optimiser settings ``does not seem deeply worrying''; this one changes the result. (4) The predictions are weakly constrained. The scaling $N\propto(\nu/\mu)^2$ is supported in sign but not decisively (slope \sharpSlope, 95\% bootstrap interval $\sharpCILo$ to $\sharpCIHi$, \sharpNMod{} modules), and the $\sim4^\circ$ offset depends on $\mu$ and $\nu$ values that are not reported: only \angleBandFrac{} of the values we scanned give 3--8$^\circ$.

\emph{Abstraction.} We agree with the level for the question ``what makes a code useful'', less so for the biological reading. The optimiser receives explicit positions and a global loss. Non-negativity and boundedness are penalties. The framework does not specify a biological circuit implementing $T(\Delta x)$. An optimal code is not shown to be learnable, or robust to noise.

\section{Significance}
\emph{Influence.} The paper gives a template: choose a variable, write down the group of its transformations, add biological and functional constraints, and read off the code. The authors already apply it to circles, spheres and 3-D space; for 3-D they suggest that irregular fields in rats and bats mean that animals give up actionability there, an intriguing claim resting on a thin argument (Appendix~M). For machine learning, the principle that a representation should let a system predict the consequences of its actions is broadly useful. The theory complements circuit models: it says which codes are useful, and mechanistic models ask whether plausible networks can implement them. Recordings showing toroidal population structure in grid modules \citep{gardner2022} make that relationship timely.

\emph{Theory--experiment loop.} The theory was motivated by known grid properties (hexagons, modules) and returns predictions, but these need sharper tests than visual comparison with \citet{stensola2012}. Three experiments follow directly. (1)~\emph{Room geometry.} Figure~5H--I already compares square-to-rectangle recordings from \citet{stensola2012}. A quantitative follow-up should record the same module across these rooms, fit any free parameters in the first room only, and predict which cells keep their scale and which shift. Separate physical walls from sampled occupancy, because the theory enters through $p(x)$. (2)~\emph{Module offsets.} Measure $\mu$ and $\nu$ for each module pair, compute the predicted offset (in our re-implementation it depends strongly on both), and compare with a null of random orientations, whose mean offset is $15^\circ$. (3)~\emph{Actionability itself.} The core assumption, one position-independent linear map per displacement, has not been tested directly. With simultaneously recorded neurons of one module, fit a map per displacement on part of the arena and predict held-out population vectors; compare its held-out performance with position-dependent and nonlinear alternatives. Success alone would not exclude those alternatives. Failures would feed back into the model, by adding sensory input or noise, as the authors suggest, or the isotropy term of \citet{gao2021}, which the authors say (Appendix~H.1) may be needed to make many modules hexagonal. Our runs, where multi-module codes were rarely hexagonal, support trying it. Because several models produce grids, experiments should target where their predictions differ, not whether grids appear.

\section{Reproducibility}
We built two implementations. Nathan's (NumPy, hand-derived gradients checked against finite differences, \nJobs{} training runs) was written from the paper's text only; the authors' code was deliberately not read. Tafara's (JAX, automatic differentiation, two reference runs and nine controlled \jDiagSteps-step runs) inspected the authors' public code (commit 940659e) when choosing initialisation and optimiser conventions, so the two used different information. On identical float64 inputs their loss terms agree to \auditLoss{} and, after aligning the functional weight, their directional derivatives to \auditGrad{}: both optimise the same equations. Table~\ref{tab:verdict} lists every claim we tested.

\emph{What held.} A single hexagonal module at large $\sigma$ (both 150k-step runs at $\sigma=\sigmaLarge$, and $\sigma=0.7$), and a module count that grows as $\sigma$ falls (Figure~\ref{fig:sigma}).

\emph{What did not.} At $\sigma=0.2$, three 150k-step runs produced \xMainLowTotalModules{} modules, none hexagonal (Figure~\ref{fig:main}). At Figure~8's $\sigma=0.4$, $l=0.5$, Tafara's three seeds gave elongated periodic cells; Nathan's two 50k-step seeds gave opposite hexagonality outcomes. At $l=0.1$, Tafara's non-hexagonal $N=64$ result agrees with the paper's band-like/square regime; Nathan's single $N=100$ run did not recover its hexagonal example. Our module-offset and 1-D harmonic-lattice findings were also incomplete (Table~\ref{tab:verdict}). These differences do not establish the frequency of success under the authors' conventions.

\emph{Constraints and measurement.} The implementations differ in functional weight, initialisation, norm target and Adam convention, so they cannot be ranked as matched training runs. With the literal target $e^4$, Nathan's norm multiplier reached its floor in all five main runs. Tight targets near 0.02 strengthened the penalty but did not guarantee satisfaction: all three JAX baseline runs exceeded 0.02 on held-out samples. Nathan's single matched $\sigma=1$ comparison lost its hexagonal module under the tighter target (Table~\ref{tab:norm}). Constraint sensitivity is therefore a hypothesis supported by limited runs, not an established explanation of failed training.

Re-evaluating five Nathan checkpoints with Tafara's fixed window and held-out samples gave norm errors of 1.85--5.83 (Table~\ref{tab:common}). Broad hexagonal examples received negative median window-based grid scores: the window contains too few repeats to assess their geometry reliably. Nathan's analysis sizes the autocorrelation window to each module's grid period; Tafara's uses one fixed window for every cell. The two scores answer different measurement questions. Negative scores here do not negate the geometric examples.

Finally, a constructed non-negative hexagonal code with nearly invariant exact Gaussian room energy had mean sampled norm penalties 0.0961, 0.0274 and 0.00644 at 50, 150 and 600 points over 128 batches (Figure~\ref{fig:control}). Valid geometry can therefore miss our 0.02 target through sampling variation. This untrained control motivates calibration; it does not prove why optimisation failed. Together, these checks support the paper's geometric possibility while identifying numerical conventions, constraint calibration and measurement scale as requirements for convincing reproduction.

\emph{Limits.} We ran one to nine seeds per setting, our thresholds for ``module'' and ``hexagonal'' are not the paper's, and grid scores are descriptive. We did not attempt room geometry, 3-D, or the paper's torus ablations. These results identify choices needed for reproduction. They do not refute the paper.
